The studio

Twelve desks, because the job has twelve parts.

Most tools take the writing seriously and lose interest at "submitted". The parts nobody builds for — the waiting, the reports you owe other people, the committee you sat on in March — are the parts that go missing when the annual review asks. Each desk is one of those parts, and each one tells you what is waiting on you.

Today

The sentence you walked away from, what arrived while you were gone, and the day's demands worst first. When there is nothing, it steps aside.

Writing

Papers in progress — plain Markdown in your own folder, or a working copy anywhere on disk. Word counts against the journal you are aiming at.

Books

Longer projects, rendered whole. Chapter word counts, a delivery date, and the publisher you owe it to.

Talks

Slide decks written as prose and exported as Beamer, reveal.js, PowerPoint, Slidy, DZSlides or a Tufte handout — each deck linked to the paper it came from.

Reading

Everything you haven't read yet, across every paper and every report — ordered by which deadline needs it first.

Plan

Where next? Word limits, typical speed and acceptance rates side by side, so choosing a venue is a decision rather than a guess.

In review

Everything you have sent out, with the Submitted → Decision trail and how long this journal usually takes.

Owed

Referee reports and comments on colleagues' and students' work — the reading other people are waiting on, with the dates you promised.

Editorial

The journals and special issues you edit: invitations out, pieces overdue, what is waiting on you rather than on someone else.

Grants

Applications you are writing and ones you are waiting to hear about, with the documents each is written in kept alongside.

Service

Vivas, tenure letters, committees, the conference you ran. One line as it happens, and by the end of the year the CV section is already written.

Notes

A scratch pad that is always there, and a notebook per paper for the plan, the objections and the seminar's pushback.

A paper's life

From the first note to the proofs.

The same paper, all the way through. Nothing is re-typed at the hand-offs, because there are no hand-offs.

Write itthe months nobody sees

An editor that knows what a paper is

Plain Markdown files in your own folder — nothing locked in a database, nothing that stops opening if this app does. Type @ to cite straight from your Zotero library. Cut a paragraph you can't quite bin and it waits in a drawer beside the paper. Bring a manuscript over from Word and its pictures come with it.

Sixteen looks for the writing surface, from a plain sheet to a green phosphor terminal; a typewriter mode; an outline rail; find-and-replace that leaves your hands where they are; and hourly snapshots of every paper, so "what did that paragraph say yesterday" has an answer.

Send itone afternoon, done properly

The submission pack, assembled for you

Pick the journal and the app puts together what that journal actually asks for: the anonymised manuscript in each required format, a separate title page, the house citation style, and the publisher's own LaTeX class where one exists. It checks your abstract and word count against the journal's own stated limits before you go near the portal.

It knows the house style of 54 journals. Exports to PDF, Word, HTML, LaTeX and PowerPoint, and twelve typeset looks of its own for the copy you read yourself.

Then waitthe bit that eats your attention

It checks the portals so you stop checking them

Paper Trail signs in to the journal's system and reads the status itself, quietly, through the day. You are told when something changes — not invited to go and look. It reads ScholarOne and Editorial Manager sites, and journals that publish no status at all can be tracked by hand.

Every paper keeps a trail of what happened and when, so three weeks of silence looks like three weeks of silence — and beside it, how long this journal usually takes. Your logins are kept in the system Keychain and used nowhere else.

Revise itwhere the real work is

A desk for the revision

The referees' points become a checklist you answer one at a time, so nothing is quietly skipped. It keeps the version you submitted alongside the one you are writing, and builds the "changes marked" copy the journal asks for — the document that is a chore to make by hand and obvious when it is missing.

When the reply is written, the response-to-referees letter is assembled from the answers you already gave.

Keep itwhat a career is made of

The record, written as it happens

Published papers keep their citation counts, checked against open indexes. Every referee report, viva, tenure letter, committee and conference goes down in one line at the time — and at the end of the year the section of the annual review that normally takes an afternoon of archaeology is already written.

Your data

It is your work, on your machine.

Where things live

  • Your drafts are plain Markdown files in your own folder. Open them in anything; delete this app and they are still there.
  • Everything the app tracks sits in one readable file on your disk. If you keep it in iCloud Drive, your own Macs sync it; nobody else's does.
  • Journal logins go in the system Keychain, encrypted by the OS, and are used only to sign in to that journal.
  • There is no account, no sign-up and no server holding your papers.

What leaves, and when

  • Signing in to a journal talks to that journal. Looking up a DOI talks to the open citation indexes. Nothing else goes out on its own.
  • Optionally, you can back your drafts up to a private GitHub repository of your own — off unless you set it up.
  • Optionally, you can contribute one anonymous record when a paper reaches a decision, so figures like "median days to first decision" come from real submissions rather than folklore. Off by default; it carries no name, no title and no manuscript ID.
