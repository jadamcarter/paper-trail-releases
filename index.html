Stop making the rounds of journal dashboards.

ScholarOne logs you out more or less as a matter of principle. So checking becomes a ritual: sign in, squint at a table, sign in somewhere else. Paper Trail runs the ritual for you.

1

Track a paper

Type the title as it appears on the journal's site and pick the journal. The app finds the cover, the manuscript ID, and the submission date on its own.

2

Add your journal login, once

Each paper's card asks for its journal login the first time. It's encrypted into your device's own secure storage, and it stays on your machine.

3

Click “Refresh all”

The app signs in, reads every status, and redraws each paper's trail. If anything has changed since you last looked, you'll get a notification.

The questions are always the same.

How long has it been? When should I expect to hear? Is this journal slow, or is it me? While a paper is out, every card on the page has an answer.

The trail

A living timeline

Submission to review to decision, drawn as one line. The marker pulses gently while reviewers have the paper, sits still through the administrative longueurs, and turns green, amber, or red when the decision lands.

Expected decisions

An honest countdown

Your submission date plus the journal's average review time gives a date you might plausibly hear by, and the card counts down to it. When that date slips past it counts up instead, in red — and offers to draft the polite chase-up email, manuscript ID and dates already filled in. Nobody enjoys writing those, which is presumably why we put them off.

Journal facts

Sourced, not folklore

Acceptance rates and wait times are mostly folklore, passed along at conferences. The card shows published figures instead: publisher metrics where journals release them, community surveys where they don't, side by side when they disagree, each linked to its source.

The journey

Every paper's biography

Papers get around. When one is rejected and resubmitted, the rejection carries forward, so the card shows the full route: where the paper has been, and how long each stop took.

There's also co-author bylines, CSV export of your submission history, decision dates as a calendar feed, and quiet automatic updates.

The paper comes back. Then the real work.

Acceptance is the exception; more often the useful question is where next? Paper Trail carries you into the next attempt while the reasons for it are still fresh — and takes the formatting off your hands.

A revise-and-resubmit is three documents, not one.

The revised paper, a copy with the changes marked, and a reply that answers every point in the referees' own numbering. Paper Trail keeps the three in one place — and builds two of them for you.

The reply

Answer each point at the moment you fix it

The decision letter arrives as a wall of text; Paper Trail splits it into the points it actually makes — the editor's letter, Referee A, Referee C — each one tickable, each with room for the line you'd write back. Those lines are the reply. Tick the last one and the whole document is on your clipboard as LaTeX or Markdown, numbered exactly as the referees numbered themselves, ready to paste into Overleaf. Points you haven't answered come out plainly marked, so nothing goes back half-finished by accident.

The marked-up copy

Changes highlighted, without marking them by hand

Most journals want a copy showing what changed, and keeping one by hand is how resubmissions go wrong. Give Paper Trail the version you submitted and the version you're revising and it typesets one: additions underlined, deletions struck through. Drop an Overleaf Source zip and it finds the main document itself — bibliography, class file and figures included, because the comparison has to compile. A paper written in Paper Trail needs nothing dropped at all: the version you sent is already in its own history. Markdown, Quarto, LaTeX and Word all go through the same machinery.

The paper itself can live wherever you actually write: in Paper Trail, in a file anywhere on your Mac, or at a link — Overleaf, a Google Doc. And one line answers the only question that matters at the deadline: ready to resubmit? paper, marked-up copy, reply — which of the three is still missing.

Write the paper here too.

Drafts is a writing studio inside the tracker: plain Markdown files in your own iCloud Drive, a calm Byword-style page to write them on, and pandoc underneath so what comes out the other end is a properly typeset paper — citations resolved, references built, house style applied.

The editor

A quiet page that knows what markdown means

One centred column on warm paper, the markdown marks fading into the page as you type. Headings become a clickable outline; ⌘F finds and replaces; typewriter scrolling and a focus mode keep the current paragraph under your eyes. Type @ and your Zotero library completes the citation; paste a DOI and the reference is fetched into Zotero and cited on the spot. Snapshots accumulate quietly as you write, so any hour of work can be got back. And the page itself is yours to dress: six writing themes — the default warm paper, an iA Writer white (real iA Writer Quattro, bundled), typewriter ivory, a green-phosphor terminal, a serif midnight, Solarized — each restyling the whole editor, chrome and all. The app around it has a night-mode switch of its own: follow the system, or pin it dark or light.

The exports

From Markdown to a typeset paper

Export to HTML, Word, or PDF via LaTeX. Citations typed as @keys come out as Author (Year) with a full References section — the bibliography is pulled from Zotero at export time, so it's never stale. Drop in your own templates once — a Word reference document, a pandoc LaTeX template, a CSL style — and every export lands in your house style. When it's time to submit, Prepare submission checks the word count against the target journal's limit and produces the anonymized PDF for blind review.

Lenses over the page

See what you've actually written

Switch on a lens and the page colours what you asked about, nothing else: adverbs, hedges and intensifiers, passive constructions, nominalizations, filler, a word you've used twice in one paragraph. Sentences that have got away from you shade as they pass thirty words, and again at forty-five. Colour only — nothing moves, nothing reflows, the caret stays exactly where it was.

One lens is on by default because the mistake it catches is silent: a citation with a full stop welded to it — @williams2020. — inside a footnote. Pandoc renders that as nothing and drops its reference from the bibliography without a warning. Paper Trail underlines it in red as you type, and “Prepare submission” counts them before you send the paper anywhere.

The paper beside the paper

Everything you need open at once

Write and read the typeset version side by side, scrolling together. Open the paper you're replying to in a reading pane in the same window, instead of a PDF covering what you're writing. A rail down the side lists your sections with a word count on each — a four-thousand-word limit is a structural problem, and this is where you see which section caused it — or lists every work you've cited, with a warning on any citekey Zotero can no longer find.

Word counts follow each journal's own stated rule, read from what the journal actually says: Analysis's 4,000 includes the notes, Episteme's 12,000 includes the notes and the references. The footer names the journals your paper has just grown out of. And History shows the paper's growth curve, compares any past version word by word, and will mark everything written since a chosen moment right there in the page.

Drafts are ordinary .md files in iCloud Drive › Paper Trail › Drafts — they sync, open in any editor, and are never locked inside the app. Because they sync, the editor also watches for the copy on your other Mac: if a paper has moved on since you opened it, the save is refused and you're shown both versions rather than quietly overwriting an afternoon's work. And a draft simply is a paper you're writing: it sits in the Writing pipeline from its first word, with live counts against the journal you're aiming at. ⌘B and ⌘I do what you expect, lists carry their own markers down, ⌥⌘F writes a footnote, and ⌘Z still reaches back through every one of them.

The rest of the desk, while you're at it.

Submissions are the visible part of the job, but not most of it. Below your cards sit five tiles — Writing, Owed, Grants, Plan, Notes — each showing a live count and a quiet warning dot when a deadline is getting close, so a glance at the strip is a glance at the week.

Writing

Every paper you're writing, one list

Write it right here — “✍️ Write in Paper Trail” starts a draft in the built-in editor — or attach an outside working copy (a file anywhere, or an Overleaf link) that opens in whatever editor you choose, Paper Trail included for Markdown files. Word counts read themselves either way; pick a target journal, and when the day comes, one click moves the paper across to tracking with the as-submitted version snapshotted.

Books

Books, at monograph scale

The same idea for the long haul: attach the master file or the whole project folder, and “Work on book” opens it in whichever editor you picked for that book — saving happens right where the book lives. Word counts sum the chapters; delivery dates warn you a month out.

Owed

What you owe everyone else

Referee reports you've promised journals and papers colleagues are waiting on, with the same countdowns as your own work and a yearly tally for the CV. PDFs annotate right inside the app; a paper shared as a Word or Google Docs link opens in the shared document itself; either way, one click drafts the email that sends your comments back.

Grants

Applications, idea to decision

One card per bid, with stages that follow the funder's own process rather than a generic pipeline. Deadlines get an extra thirty-day warning — grant deadlines being what they are — and each card keeps the working folder and the funder's portal one click away.

Notes

A scratch pad that files nothing

One page for the things that don't belong anywhere — what an editor said on the phone, the argument you don't want to lose on the walk home. It saves itself as you type, and citations typed with @ read back as Author (Year), like a page of prose.

Entry forms live behind one ＋ Add menu; exports, backups and settings behind a single ⋯ — and ⌘K opens a palette that jumps to any paper, draft, report or grant by name. The page below your cards stays quiet.

It meets your other tools where they are.

A tracker earns its keep by talking to the things you already trust — your calendar, and your reference manager. Both connections run entirely on your machine.

Calendar integration

Your deadlines, in your actual calendar

Every date Paper Trail knows — revision deadlines, expected decisions, referee reports, ready-by dates, grant deadlines — written into a calendar you choose and kept there: change a due date and its event moves, remove the paper and the event goes with it. Pick a calendar that syncs and the dates reach your phone too. Mac only for now; on Windows, a one-click .ics export does the same by hand.

Zotero integration

Your library, one @ away

Search your Zotero library from inside the app and open any paper in Zotero's own reader — highlights stay in Zotero, where they belong. Search results say before you click whether the item actually has a PDF attached, and when it hasn't, one button looks for a legal open-access copy instead. Typing @ in a draft or a note looks up your library as you type and cites as you pick: Author (Year) while you read, the Better BibTeX citation key underneath. Paste a DOI and the reference is fetched into Zotero and cited on the spot; at export time the bibliography is pulled fresh, so the References section is never stale. Needs Zotero with the free Better BibTeX plugin.

And when the paper isn't in your library yet: type @ and a name that isn't there, and instead of the completer simply giving up it offers to go and look: a Crossref search, right in the popover. Pick the paper and it's saved into Zotero and cited in the sentence you were already writing — the pasted-DOI trick, without needing to have found the DOI. There's a search box in the Zotero panel for browsing rather than citing, too. Google Scholar and PhilPapers can't be searched from inside an app — neither offers a way in — so those two open in your browser with the query already filled, where your own Zotero connector does the saving.

Your data stays on your machine.

Paper Trail is a desktop app in the old-fashioned sense. There's no account to create and no server holding your submission history.

  • Your logins never leave your device. Journal passwords are encrypted into the operating system's secure storage (the Mac Keychain, or Windows's equivalent) and used only to sign in from your computer, the way you would.
  • Nothing is stored on anyone else's server. Your tracking data is one plain JSON file, and your drafts are ordinary Markdown files — both kept in your own iCloud Drive when you have it (so a second Mac just works), on your disk when you don't. Delete the app and everything is still yours, readable, in Finder.
  • Community data is opt-in, and you can read it first. If, and only if, you switch it on, each concluded submission contributes one anonymous record to shared journal statistics. The record is reproduced here in full; there is nothing else.

Paper Trail does not send your manuscript titles, manuscript IDs, co-author names, journal statuses or journal passwords to us. The app communicates directly with journal websites to retrieve information, and with doi.org only when you paste a DOI to import a reference. Our download, update and community-statistics providers may receive ordinary technical connection data, as explained in the Privacy Notice.
